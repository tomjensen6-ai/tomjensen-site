Operational Frameworks

Simple models for deciding how important a system is, who gets called, and how much control a change needs.

Purpose

How criticality, escalation and change types make decisions the same on every shift.

Why frameworks matter

Procedures say what to do. Frameworks decide how serious something is, who must be involved and how fast.

Without them, the answer depends on who is on shift. One person escalates early, another waits. The same job gets a full MOP one week and a quick fix the next.

1. Criticality

Criticality is how bad it is if an asset fails. It is not the same as risk. Risk also counts how likely the failure is: see Risk Assessment.

Base it on what a failure would do, not on what the equipment cost.

Level If it fails Examples Control
Critical People are at risk, or the critical load is lost or exposed Utility supply, transfer switches, UPS, generators, main cooling, fire systems Tested procedures, approval, set escalation, yearly review
High Redundancy, capacity or security is reduced A redundant cooling unit, access control, leak detection Approved procedures, a risk review, a named owner
Medium Local work or support functions are affected Local sensors, non-critical panels, room lighting Standard procedures, periodic review
Low Little effect on operations Accessories, office equipment Basic documentation, a named owner

2. Escalation

Escalate on impact, safety, lost redundancy and uncertainty. Don't wait for a full failure if you don't know what state the site is in.

Level When Who
1. Local Known minor issue, no impact, no safety concern, a procedure exists Operator on duty handles it and logs it
2. Attention Redundancy reduced, repeated alarms, unclear cause Shift lead takes over, assigns an owner, increases monitoring
3. Incident A critical system is affected, customer impact is possible, or the state is unclear Incident lead takes over. The Facility manager or On-call person is called
4. Emergency People at risk, major service impact, or several critical systems affected Emergency services first if people are at risk. Management is told

The levels are a starting point. During an incident, the Incident lead can move it up or down as the situation changes.

3. Change types

Classify planned work by what it can affect, not by how routine it feels. A job done a hundred times is still major if it removes redundancy.

Type When Control
Minor No effect on critical systems, no redundancy lost, simple to undo SOP, operations team approval, normal log
Normal Planned work with limited exposure and a known procedure MOP, operations team approval, verification
Major Redundancy reduced, a critical system involved, or customers affected MOP with hold points and rollback, Facility manager approval, customer notice
Emergency Urgent work to restore or protect operations A manager on site or by phone approves, reached through the escalation list. Logged and reviewed afterwards

The types are my own, close to ITIL's standard, normal and emergency changes.

If rollback is unclear, the change is not ready.

Request → Risk review → Approval → Execution → Verification → Closeout

4. Documentation hierarchy

Each level governs the one below it. A policy doesn't hold every step, and an SOP doesn't override the policy.

Level Document What it does
1 Policy Sets authority, ownership and the rules that must be followed
2 Frameworks Models for criticality, escalation, change and risk
3 SOP, MOP, EOP Routine work, planned work and emergency response
4 Checklists Task-level steps and checks
5 Records and logs Show what was done, when and by whom

How documents are owned, approved, reviewed and retired is on the Governance page.
