Utility Power Loss

An example emergency procedure for the first hours after the utility supply is lost. People first, then the power path, then time: battery, cooling and fuel.

Classification

An example I wrote for this site. A real procedure must follow the site's design, its emergency plan and local rules.

Document ID: EOP-PWR-001
Revision: 2.0
Status: Example
Activation: Loss of utility supply, full or partial

1. Purpose

What to do when the utility supply is lost: keep people safe, confirm the load is carried, and watch the three clocks: battery, cooling and fuel.

2. Scope

Full or partial loss of the utility supply, including loss of one of two feeds. It does not replace switching procedures, evacuation plans or the manufacturer's instructions.

3. When to use it

  • Utility supply lost, fully or on one feed
  • UPS running on battery
  • Generators started without a planned test
  • Unexpected transfer or abnormal electrical state
  • You cannot tell what feeds the load

4. The three clocks

When the utility goes, time starts running on three things:

  • Battery: if the generators do not take the load, the UPS batteries usually last minutes, not hours.
  • Cooling: chillers and cooling units may stop during the transfer and need time to restart. Meanwhile, temperatures rise, fast in dense rooms.
  • Fuel: on generators, how long you can run depends on fuel on site and the load. In a long or regional outage, deliveries can be slow.

Know your numbers before an outage: battery runtime at current load, cooling restart time and fuel hours at current load.

5. First steps

Step Action Check Owner
1 Check people: anyone hurt, stuck in a lift, or in an area without emergency lighting. People safe, or help called. Operator on duty / Security
2 Read the power state in BMS/EPMS: utility, UPS, generators, transfer switches. Power path known: what feeds the load now. Operator on duty
3 Confirm the generators started and took the load. If not, start them as the site procedure says, and escalate at once. Load on generator, or escalation started. Operator on duty
4 Check the UPS: on battery or back on input, battery runtime left, alarms. Runtime known and logged. Operator on duty
5 Check that cooling restarted: chillers, cooling units, pumps. Watch room temperatures. Cooling running, temperatures stable. Operator on duty / Facility operations
6 The incident lead named in the site's emergency plan takes charge. Call in the on-call person and specialists as needed. If you are alone, you lead until someone takes over. Everyone knows who is in charge. Incident lead
7 Find out if the cause is outside or inside the site: call the grid operator, check your own switchgear. Ask how long the outage may last. Cause and expected duration known, or logged as unknown. Incident lead / Electrical responsible person
8 Check fuel: level, use at current load, hours left. Call the fuel supplier early if the outage may be long. Fuel hours known. Delivery agreed if needed. Facility operations
9 Walk the generator room: no leaks, smell, unusual noise or alarms. Check ventilation. Generators running normally. Operator on duty / Generator service company
10 Start the incident log. Inform stakeholders as the communication plan says. Set the time for the next update. Log running, updates sent. Incident lead / Recorder

Staffing: With one person on site, steps 1 to 5 come first. Call in help early. A long outage is too much for one person.

6. Escalate at once if

  • A generator fails to start, stops, or cannot take the load
  • Battery runtime is short or falling faster than expected
  • Room temperatures keep rising
  • Fuel hours fall below the site's limit, with no delivery confirmed
  • The grid operator expects a long outage
  • Smoke, a burning smell, a fire alarm or signs of arcing
  • You cannot tell what feeds the load
  • Redundancy falls below the site's limit

7. Updates

One person leads. If possible, someone else handles communication.

Keep updates short, factual and timed: Time — power state — impact — actions — fuel hours — next update.

8. Back to utility

Do not rush back. A grid that has just come back can fail again.

  • Wait until the utility has been stable for the time the site design sets.
  • Transfer back in a controlled way, when the incident lead decides.
  • Let the generators run their cool-down and return to standby.
  • Check that the UPS is back on normal input and the batteries are charging. Until they are full, runtime is reduced, so avoid planned work.
  • Refuel the generators.
  • Check cooling and all alarms.
  • Write the incident report, with what we learned.
