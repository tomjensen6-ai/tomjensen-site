Cooling Failure Response

An example emergency procedure for when cooling is lost or temperatures start to rise. People first, then the cooling state, then the clock.

Classification

An example I wrote for this site. A real procedure must follow the site’s design, its emergency plan and local rules.

Document ID: EOP-CLG-002
Revision: 2.0
Status: Example
Activation: Loss of cooling or rising temperatures

1. Purpose

What to do when cooling fails or temperatures rise without a known reason: keep people safe, find out what has stopped, and know how long the room has before it reaches its limit.

2. The real risk

Cooling failures come in two sizes. One unit stops, the standby unit takes over, and the room loses its redundancy but not its temperature. Or several units or the chiller plant stop, and the temperature starts to climb at once.

In the second case, the clock decides everything. How fast the room heats depends on the load and how much air and water is still moving. In a dense room it can be minutes before IT equipment starts to throttle or shut down. Know your room’s numbers before it happens.

The room average can fool you. One area can overheat while the total looks fine, if the units that are left can’t push enough air there. Check each area, not only the room.

A power blip is a common cause. Chillers that stop can take several minutes to restart, and the room heats while you wait. Some designs bridge the gap with cold water storage tanks. UPS on the pumps and on the chiller controls also shortens the restart. Test the restart and tune it, so the gap is as short as it can be.

Other causes are a pump or chiller fault, a control fault, a unit left in local mode after maintenance, and a leak that forces valves closed.

In a modern data hall with high load, open doors and portable coolers won’t hold the temperature for long. Portable units can help in small rooms with low load, such as network rooms. In a data hall, what protects you is redundancy that is built in and tested often. If cooling can’t be restored in time, a controlled shutdown of IT equipment is the only way left to save it.

The people responding face hazards too. Hot aisles get very hot fast. A refrigerant leak can push out the air in a closed plant room, so know the alarm levels before you go in. A leak puts water near electrical equipment.

3. When to use it

  • Temperature rising in a data hall or technical room without a known reason
  • One or more cooling units stopped or in alarm
  • Standby unit did not start
  • Chilled water plant in alarm: chiller, pump or flow
  • Cooling stopped after a power interruption
  • Temperature or humidity alarms from IT equipment or customers

4. Who does what

Role Does
Shift lead (incident lead) Leads the response, decides on escalation and informs others.
Operator on duty First checks in BMS and on site. Makes the adjustments they are authorized to make. Watches temperatures and trends.
On-call person Comes to site or supports by phone when called.
Cooling vendor specialist Finds and fixes the fault in the cooling equipment.
Facility manager Informed when the room loses redundancy or a limit is close.
Recorder Keeps the timeline: alarms, temperatures, actions and decisions.
Security Lets the vendor in quickly and keeps access to the room to those who need it.

Staffing:

  • No one on site: the alarm goes to the On-call person, who checks remotely, calls the vendor and comes to site.
  • One person on site: the Operator on duty does the first checks and the adjustments they are authorized to make, and calls the Shift lead or On-call person and the vendor at once. A cooling fault usually has more tasks than one person can handle, so call for help early.
  • Two or more on site: one stays in BMS and watches the trend. The others check the rooms, the plant room and the cooling equipment.

5. First steps

Step Action Check Owner
1 Check people: anyone in a hot aisle, plant room or near water. People safe, or help called. Operator on duty
2 Read the cooling state in BMS: which units run, which stopped, standby units, chilled water supply. What has stopped is known. Operator on duty
3 Check temperatures per area and the trend. Hottest area and how fast it rises is known. Operator on duty
4 Call the Shift lead and the cooling vendor. Both informed, vendor on the way. Operator on duty
5 Check that standby units started. Start them by hand if they didn’t and you are authorized to. All available units running. Operator on duty
6 Check the room: doors closed, containment in place, floor tiles in place, no blocked air paths. Air goes where it should. Operator on duty
7 Estimate the time to the room’s limit at the current trend. Time known and told to the Shift lead. Shift lead
8 Decide the update interval and who is informed. Interval set, first update sent. Shift lead
9 Start the timeline. Alarms, temperatures, actions and decisions recorded. Recorder

Note: What counts as the room’s limit varies. It is set by the site design and what has been agreed with customers. ASHRAE TC 9.9 gives a recommended inlet range of 18⁠–⁠27 °C for most IT equipment, and wider allowable ranges.

6. If it gets worse

If the temperature keeps rising and the cause isn’t fixed within the time you have, escalate. Don’t wait for the limit.

More units fail, or the trend gets steeper: the Shift lead escalates to the Facility manager and informs customers following the site’s own plan.

Water on the floor or a leak alarm: follow Water Leak Response (EOP-WTR-003).

Cooling stopped because power was lost: follow Utility Power Loss Response (EOP-PWR-001). Power comes first. Cooling restarts when the power path is stable.

Fire alarm, smoke or a burning smell: follow Fire Alarm Response (EOP-FIR-004). Cooling stays on. Dampers may close and change the air paths, so watch the temperatures.

Monitoring lost: check temperatures in the room by hand, and treat the trend as rising until you know.

Cooling can’t be restored before the limit: a controlled shutdown of IT equipment is the last resort. It is decided with the customers, following the site’s own plan, and done before equipment shuts itself down.

Someone is hurt: people first. Call for help and follow the site’s own plan.

7. Back to normal

  • All units in automatic, running or on standby as designed
  • Standby units back on standby
  • Temperatures stable at the baseline for the time set by the site
  • No alarms active or suppressed
  • Containment and floor tiles in place
  • Timeline complete, cause found or a plan to find it
  • Review held: what failed, what worked, what changes

9. Rules and standards

ASHRAE TC 9.9 sets the thermal guidelines most data centers use for IT inlet temperatures. In Europe, the F-gas Regulation means only certified people may work on refrigerant circuits. Electrical work during the response follows the local rules: EN 50110-1 in Europe, FSE in Norway, the Electricity at Work Regulations 1989 in the UK and NFPA 70E in the US.
