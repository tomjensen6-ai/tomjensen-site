Water Leak Response

An example emergency procedure for water where it shouldn’t be. People first, then find the source, then stop the water without stopping the cooling by mistake.

Classification

An example I wrote for this site. A real procedure must follow the site’s design, its emergency plan and local rules.

Document ID: EOP-WTR-003
Revision: 2.0
Status: Example
Activation: Leak alarm or water found

1. Purpose

What to do when a leak alarm goes off or water is found: keep people safe, find the source, stop the water, and protect the equipment and the cooling.

2. The real risk

Water and electricity are the first danger, and it is a danger to people. Don’t step into water near energized equipment, and don’t touch wet equipment.

The leak alarm tells you where the water arrived, not where it came from. Under a raised floor or along cable trays, water can travel a long way before anyone sees it. Follow it back to the source.

In a data hall, the water usually comes from the cooling. Condensate is the most common source: drains that block, pans that overflow, and cold pipes that sweat. Chilled water pipes, valves and coils come next. Closing a valve stops the leak, but it can also stop cooling to every unit on that circuit. Know which units you lose before you close it. If you lose more than the room can spare, it is now a cooling failure too.

Water can also come from outside the cooling: the roof, the facade, a drain that backs up, or pipes from rooms above. These are harder to stop, because the valve may not be yours.

Most leaks can be prevented. Keep the chilled water on the cold side at a temperature that doesn’t make pipes and coils sweat, and keep the pipes insulated. Don’t bring a pressurized water feed into a data hall for a portable humidifier. Keep full control of the roof: regular checks, planned maintenance, and ideally a slight slope so water doesn’t stand.

To find the water under a raised floor, you often have to lift tiles. Each open tile changes the airflow. Lift what you need, and put tiles back as soon as you can.

3. When to use it

  • Leak detection alarm
  • Water seen on the floor, under the floor, in a ceiling or on equipment
  • Condensate drain or pump alarm with water outside the unit
  • Water coming in from the roof, facade or rooms above
  • Unexplained damp or drips near electrical equipment

4. Who does what

Role Does
Shift lead (incident lead) Leads the response, decides on escalation and informs others.
Operator on duty Checks the alarm, finds the water, closes the isolation valve, keeps people away from wet equipment.
On-call person Comes to site or supports by phone when called.
Electrical responsible person Decides whether power in a wet area is isolated, and when wet equipment may be energized again.
Vendor specialist (cooling or plumbing) Finds and repairs the leak.
Facility manager Informed when water reaches equipment or the source is outside the site’s control.
Recorder Keeps the timeline: alarms, actions and decisions.
Security Lets vendors in quickly and keeps people out of wet areas.

Staffing:

  • No one on site: the alarm goes to the On-call person, who checks remotely and comes to site.
  • One person on site: the Operator on duty is usually the first responder. Find the water and stop it if it is safe, and call the Shift lead or On-call person at once. Don’t go into water near energized equipment alone. A major leak is more than one person can handle, so call for help early.
  • Two or more on site: one watches BMS and the cooling. The others find and stop the water.

5. First steps

Step Action Check Owner
1 Check people: anyone in the wet area, near wet equipment, or under a sagging ceiling. People safe and away from the water, or help called. Operator on duty
2 Read the leak alarm in BMS or the leak detection panel: which zone and where on the cable. Location of the alarm known. Operator on duty
3 Go and look. Stay out of water near energized equipment. Water found, and how much. Operator on duty
4 Call the Shift lead. Shift lead informed. Operator on duty
5 Follow the water back to the source. Lift floor tiles only where needed. Source found, or search area narrowed. Operator on duty
6 Before closing a valve, check which cooling units it feeds. Effect on cooling known. Operator on duty
7 Stop the water: close the isolation valve if it is safe. Water no longer spreading. Operator on duty
8 Keep water away from power: absorbent material or barriers, never by hand on energized equipment. Water contained. Operator on duty
9 Call the vendor. Vendor on the way. Shift lead
10 Watch cooling and temperatures in BMS while valves are closed. Temperatures stable. Operator on duty
11 Start the timeline. Alarms, actions and decisions recorded. Recorder

Note: Where the isolation valves sit and what each one feeds vary between sites. Everyone on duty should know where they are and have practiced closing them before they are needed.

6. If it gets worse

If water reaches energized equipment, keep everyone away and call the Electrical responsible person. Nobody touches it until they decide.

Water still threatens energized equipment: the Electrical responsible person has it powered down. This is rare. Critical panels should have their own protection against leaks. When it is needed, people come first, then the installation.

Closing the valve stops cooling to more units than the room can spare: follow Cooling Failure Response (EOP-CLG-002).

The source can’t be found or stopped: keep searching, contain what you can, and the Shift lead escalates to the Facility manager.

The ceiling sags or the structure looks damaged: keep people out of the area and escalate.

A sprinkler head has gone off, or there is smoke: follow Fire Alarm Response (EOP-FIR-004).

Power is lost in the area: follow Utility Power Loss Response (EOP-PWR-001).

Someone is hurt: people first. Call for help and follow the site’s own plan.

7. Back to normal

  • Leak repaired, valves in their normal position
  • Cooling back to normal, temperatures at the baseline
  • Area dry, under the floor and in cable trays too
  • Wet equipment checked before it is energized again
  • Leak detection cable dried and tested with water
  • Floor tiles back, containment in place
  • Timeline complete, cause found or a plan to find it
  • Review held: what failed, what worked, what changes

9. Rules and standards

Work on and near electrical installations, including deciding whether to isolate a wet area, follows the local rules: EN 50110-1 in Europe, FSE in Norway, the Electricity at Work Regulations 1989 in the UK and NFPA 70E in the US. Repairs to cooling pipework follow the manufacturer’s instructions and, for refrigerant circuits in Europe, the F-gas Regulation.
