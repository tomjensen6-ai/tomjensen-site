Operating Principles

Five rules I work by, and what each one means in daily work. You will find them in every procedure in this library.

01

Reliability before complexity

A more advanced solution is only better if it makes the site more reliable. Every extra part adds something: another dependency, another way to fail, more training, more documents, and more for a tired person to misunderstand at 3 a.m.

I prefer solutions people can understand and run. Automation and monitoring help when they give better control. They hurt when they hide a weak process.

In practice

  • Remove dependencies you don't need.
  • Check that staff can run it when something is wrong, not only on a normal day.
  • Keep a manual way to operate anything whose failure would hurt.
  • Ask whether new technology removes a risk or just moves it out of sight.
02

Clear ownership

When nobody owns something, small problems stay open, temporary fixes become permanent, and you find out who was responsible during an incident or an audit.

Every system, procedure and open action needs a named owner, a known way to escalate, and someone who follows up. Good ownership has nothing to do with blame. It makes operations calmer, because people know who decides, who checks and who must be told.

In practice

  • Name an owner for every system, procedure and recurring check.
  • Make approval roles for planned work clear.
  • Let a second person verify critical steps.
  • Track open actions until they are closed, not just discussed.
03

Stay calm under pressure

Pressure comes with the job. The aim is to keep it from turning into chaos, and that work happens before the incident: procedures, training, escalation rules and an agreed way to talk.

When something goes wrong, people need a few simple questions they already know. What is the state now? What is the immediate risk? Who leads? What must not be touched? What is the next safe step?

In practice

  • Keep emergency procedures short, direct and built around roles.
  • Agree escalation limits before anything happens.
  • Keep updates factual: status, risk, action, owner, time.
  • Drill situations where people must stop, check and decide.
04

Documentation that gets used

A document only has value if it helps someone do the job safely and the same way each time. An outdated, vague or hard-to-find document can be worse than none, because people trust it.

SOPs, MOPs, EOPs, risk assessments and asset registers should point to each other. The aim is to keep what the team knows and make the right action repeatable. Paperwork for its own sake helps nobody.

In practice

  • Give every document an owner, a version, an approval date and a review date.
  • Write procedures for the actual task, step by step.
  • Include what must be true before you start, the risks, the checks and the way back.
  • Archive documents that are no longer valid.
05

Keep it maintainable

A system isn't finished when it is installed. It has to be run, patched, tested, inspected, renewed and one day replaced. If it can't be maintained properly, it gets less reliable every year, however good the design was.

The best choice is often the one trained people can keep stable for years: spare parts, vendor access, service routines, monitoring, test schedules, and people who know how it works.

In practice

  • Look at lifetime cost and support before choosing technology.
  • Keep maintenance visible in plans and budgets.
  • Document dependencies, settings, access and how to recover.
  • Check that the team still knows how to run it safely.

How they connect

The principles depend on each other. Unclear ownership weakens reliability. Documents lose value when nobody maintains them. Technology gets risky when only one person understands it. Incidents get harder when change history, asset information and escalation paths are missing.

Area What should be clear
Assets What exists, where it is, who owns it, and how it is maintained.
Procedures How normal, planned and emergency work is done and checked.
Risk What can fail, what happens then, and what controls are in place.
Change What is changing, who approved it, who is involved, and the way back.
Continuity How the service is protected or restored when something fails.

Where these ideas come from

The same ideas run through the common management standards: ISO 31000 for risk management, ISO 55001 for asset management and ISO 22301 for business continuity. This page doesn't follow any of them to the letter, but they are good further reading.

These are general rules. They don't replace site rules, law, safety regulations, manufacturer instructions or a proper risk assessment.
