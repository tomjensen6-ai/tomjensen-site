Planned Utility Transfer Test

How I would run a planned test where the site moves from utility power to generators and back. The point is to find weak spots on a day you chose, with people in place, before a real outage finds them for you.

Classification

An example I wrote for this site. Real switching must follow the manufacturer's procedure, the site design and local electrical safety rules.

Document ID: MOP-PWR-002
Revision: 2.0
Status: Example
Approval: Example only

1. Purpose

This test checks the whole chain: the utility loss is detected, the generators start and take load, the UPS carries the IT load through the gap, cooling restarts, and the site returns to utility without trouble.

It also tests people. Does everyone know their task, can they see what is happening, and do they stop when something looks wrong?

2. The real risk

During the test you give up your normal power source on purpose. Three things can go wrong.

The automation fails and you can't get back fast. The controller, transfer switch or breaker logic does something unexpected, and the way back depends on someone switching by hand. This is the worst case. If that person isn't trained, or isn't in the building, a short test becomes a long outage. Manual switching has to be drilled before the test, not learned during it. In a real outage you don't choose the hour. At night the specialists may still be on their way, so the people on site must be able to switch by hand alone.

A generator fails to start or trips. The UPS batteries hold the IT load for minutes, not hours.

Cooling doesn't come back quickly. This is the one most likely to happen. Most sites transfer with a short break (open transition). The UPS carries the IT load through it, but anything not on UPS stops and has to restart on generator, and again when you go back to utility. Chillers can take several minutes to restart, and the data halls warm up meanwhile.

Some sites put pumps, valves and CRAH fans on their own UPS, so water and air keep moving through the break. That helps a lot. The chillers still have to restart.

Note: Some sites go back to utility with a closed transition. The generator runs in parallel with the grid for a moment, so the load sees no second break. This needs synchronising equipment, protection, and approval from the grid company. Know which method your site uses before you start.

3. How the test is started

There are three common ways:

  • Open the utility incoming breaker by hand. Simple, and close to a real outage.
  • Make the generator controller or power management system see a mains failure, for example by removing its mains voltage signal. The system then trips the incoming breaker itself and runs its normal sequence. This tests the automatic chain the way a real outage would. Take the steps from the system supplier, and put the signal back exactly as it was afterwards.
  • Use the test function on the transfer switch. Gentler, but depending on the design it may not test the full chain.

Pick the method on purpose and write it in the plan. The way back depends on it. If you opened a breaker, someone has to close it. If you removed a signal, someone has to restore it.

4. Before the test

  • ☐ Change approved, with a test window and a written switching sequence
  • ☐ The Electrical responsible person has approved the switching sequence
  • ☐ Test lead named in the plan, and everyone knows who can call stop
  • ☐ Incident categories agreed: which events mean a full return to utility, and who makes the call
  • ☐ No other work going on, and nothing else reducing redundancy (no UPS on bypass, no generator out of service)
  • ☐ Weather and grid checked: no storms forecast, no planned grid work nearby
  • ☐ Test timed for when specialists are on site, never at night with support on call only
  • ☐ Manual switching procedure up to date, and drilled by the people who will be on site
  • ☐ Automation specialist on site for the test
  • ☐ High-voltage sites: everyone who will enter switch rooms or generator rooms has documented access, and the person responsible for the high-voltage installation has approved the plan
  • ☐ Generators in automatic, no alarms, fuel above the site minimum, engine heaters working, starter batteries charged
  • ☐ UPS: all modules online, not on bypass, batteries charged, no alarms, recent battery test results good
  • ☐ ATS and switchgear in automatic with no alarms; retransfer delay and generator cooldown settings known
  • ☐ Cooling: all units running, temperatures noted as a baseline, chiller restart time known
  • ☐ BMS, EPMS and DCIM logging, with one person assigned to watch them
  • ☐ Security and the alarm centre told that alarms during the test are expected
  • ☐ Customers and others informed according to the communication plan
  • ☐ Radios or phones tested, with one agreed channel for the test

5. Who does what

Role Task
Test lead (named in the plan; often the Shift lead or Facility manager) Runs the briefing, gives the go at each hold point, calls stop.
Electrical responsible person Does or supervises all switching, under the local electrical safety rules.
Person responsible for the high-voltage installation (if the site has one) Approves the plan and who may enter and switch in high-voltage rooms.
Automation specialist (vendor or in-house) On site. Knows the controller and power management logic. Restores any test signals.
Operator on duty Watches BMS, EPMS and DCIM. Logs times and values.
Generator specialist (vendor) At the generators: local checks, start, load, cooldown.
UPS specialist (vendor, if used) Watches the UPS during both transfers.
Change manager Approvals, notifications and records.
Security Keeps access open for the test team, routes alarm-centre calls to the Test lead.
On-call person Told about the test and reachable throughout.

Staffing:

  • No one on site: never run this test. Remote start being possible does not change that.
  • One person: don't run it. One person cannot watch the switchgear, the UPS and the cooling at once and still make the call to stop.
  • Two or more: the minimum. The Electrical responsible person at the switchgear, at least one person watching UPS and cooling, and the Test lead in one place with the overview. Vendor specialists at the generators where you have them.
  • Security: told in advance. They know alarms are expected, keep routes to the switch rooms and generators open, and let no one walk into the generator area unannounced while the test runs.
  • High voltage: if generators or switchgear run above 1000 V AC, stricter rules apply to who may enter the rooms and who may switch. In Norway, FSE requires routines for access to electrical installations, and a documented record of who has access to high-voltage installations. Operators and security often may not enter these rooms alone. Plan every task, including the manual way back, so it is done by someone allowed to do it.

6. Steps

Step Action Check Owner
1 Brief everyone: method, sequence, expected behaviour, stop rule, incident categories, radio channel. Everyone can say their own task and who calls stop. Test lead
2 Confirm the change is approved and nothing else is going on. No other work. No reduced redundancy. Test lead, Change manager
3 Check the generators locally. In automatic, no alarms, fuel and heaters OK. Generator specialist
4 Check the UPS. All modules online, not on bypass, no alarms. UPS specialist or Operator on duty
5 Check the ATS and switchgear. In automatic, utility present, no alarms. Electrical responsible person
6 Check cooling and note start temperatures. All units running. Baseline recorded. Operator on duty
7 Check monitoring and note start values: load, voltage, frequency. Logging active. Values recorded. Operator on duty
8 Hold point: the Test lead asks each person if they are ready. Every role confirms. No go without all of them. Test lead
9 Start the test with the approved method. Note the time. Utility supply lost, or mains failure seen by the controller, as planned. Electrical responsible person, Automation specialist
10 Watch the generators start and take load. Note the time to start and to take load. Within the times the site expects. Generator specialist
11 Watch the UPS. It runs on battery during the gap, then goes back to normal on generator power. Battery discharge stops after transfer. No module alarms. UPS specialist or Operator on duty
12 Watch cooling restart. All units back. Temperatures within the site limit. Operator on duty
13 Run on generator for the planned time. Log load, voltage, frequency and temperatures at the agreed interval. Stable values. Only expected alarms. Operator on duty
14 Hold point: before returning, check that the utility side is healthy. Utility voltage present and normal. Test lead gives the go. Electrical responsible person, Test lead
15 Return to utility with the approved sequence. Expect the retransfer delay and, with open transition, a second short break. Load back on utility. UPS back to normal. Electrical responsible person
16 If a signal was removed to start the test, restore it exactly as it was. Controller shows mains normal, no test alarms left, back in automatic. Automation specialist
17 Watch the generators cool down and return to standby. Generators back in automatic. A generator left in manual or off after a test is a common miss. Generator specialist
18 Walk round: switchgear, UPS, cooling. All breakers in their normal position. No new alarms. Cooling stable. Electrical responsible person, Operator on duty
19 Record the result and close the change. Times, values, alarms and follow-ups recorded. Test lead, Change manager

7. If it goes wrong during the test

Before the test, agree which events mean a full return to utility. My list: any generator failure, any UPS alarm that isn't expected, any cooling limit passed, any automation fault, anyone hurt. Smaller deviations are written down and looked at afterwards.

Anyone can call stop. When someone does, the default is to return to utility, then find out why.

Generator does not start or does not take load: the UPS is on battery and the clock is running. Return to utility at once: close the breaker you opened, restore the signal, or end the test mode. Don't troubleshoot first.

Generator trips after taking load: same. If you opened the utility breaker or removed the mains signal, the system cannot go back on its own. Someone has to close that breaker or restore that signal.

One of several generators fails and the rest carry the load: the Test lead decides. My default is still to go back to utility and investigate on a normal day.

UPS alarm, a module trips, or the battery keeps discharging after the generators took load: stop and return to utility.

Cooling does not restart, or temperatures pass the site limit: stop, return to utility and restart cooling by the site's own procedure. Don't wait for temperatures to recover on generator.

The automation does not respond: the controller or transfer switch won't go back, or a breaker won't close from the control system. Switch by hand, using the drilled procedure. This is where training matters most. The manual way back only works if the right person is in the right room and has done it before.

The grid fails for real during the test: there is no way back to utility. You are in a real outage now. Switch to Utility Power Loss Response (EOP-PWR-001), with the people already on site.

Someone is hurt, or there is smoke or fire: people first. Stop the test and call for help. Follow the site's fire plan; see Fire Alarm Response (EOP-FIR-004).

If the site cannot get back to its normal state, treat it as an incident under the site's own plan.

8. After the test

  • ☐ Load on utility, all breakers in their normal position
  • ☐ Any test signals restored, controller back in automatic
  • ☐ Generators in automatic and ready, fuel noted
  • ☐ UPS normal, batteries recharging, no alarms
  • ☐ ATS and switchgear in automatic
  • ☐ Cooling normal, temperatures back to baseline
  • ☐ Alarms reviewed; every unexpected one explained or turned into a follow-up
  • ☐ Times recorded: utility loss, generator start, load taken, return to utility
  • ☐ Anything that did not go as expected written down, with an owner
  • ☐ Everyone informed that the test is finished

The times you record are the most useful part of the test. Compare them with the last test. A generator that takes longer to take load each time is telling you something.

9. Rules and standards

The rules for this test vary by country and by site. In the US, NFPA 110 (Standard for Emergency and Standby Power Systems) covers these systems, including how they are tested. For work on the electrical installation itself, Europe has EN 50110-1, Norway has FSE (Forskrift om sikkerhet ved arbeid i og drift av elektriske anlegg), the UK has the Electricity at Work Regulations 1989 and the US has NFPA 70E. Generator makers, insurers and the site's own design add their own requirements. Where they differ from this example, they win.
