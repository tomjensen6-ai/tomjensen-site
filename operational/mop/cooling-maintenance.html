CRAC/CRAH Planned Maintenance

How I would take a cooling unit out of service for maintenance and bring it back. The work itself belongs to the specialist. Operations owns the room while the unit is out, and the moment it goes back.

Classification

An example I wrote for this site. Real maintenance must follow the manufacturer’s procedure, the site design and local safety rules.

Document ID: MOP-CLG-003
Revision: 2.0
Status: Example
Approval: Example only

1. Purpose

Take one cooling unit out of service for planned maintenance while the rest of the cooling carries the room, then return it to service in automatic, with the room as it was before.

2. The real risk

While the unit is out, the room has less cooling and usually no redundancy left. If another unit fails during the work, temperatures start to rise, and the time you have depends on the load and how far the room is from its limits.

Redundancy is designed per room or per area, depending on the building. In a colo with several rooms, it is per room. Either way, check that the units that are left can reach the area around the stopped unit, not only that the total is enough. Otherwise a hot spot can appear next to it.

Returning the unit to service is the second danger. Units are left in local mode, chilled water valves are left closed, alarms are left suppressed, and a standby unit started by hand is left running instead of back on standby. Each one looks fine on the day and removes redundancy for weeks.

Before the unit comes out, know how long it takes to put it back. That time decides how fast you can react if something else fails.

Note: On chilled-water units (CRAH), the work may need the water valves closed. On direct-expansion units (CRAC), only a certified technician works on the refrigerant circuit.

3. Before the work

  • ☐ Change approved, with a time window and the exact unit named
  • ☐ No other work going on that reduces cooling or power redundancy
  • ☐ The remaining units can carry the current load, including the area around the unit that comes out
  • ☐ The standby unit is in automatic, without alarms, and ready to start on its own
  • ☐ Room temperatures stable and well inside the site’s limits; no cooling, leak or humidity alarms
  • ☐ Parts, filters and tools on site before the unit is stopped
  • ☐ The time needed to return the unit to service is known
  • ☐ Stop conditions agreed: the temperature limit, and what happens if another unit fails
  • ☐ Vendor and Electrical responsible person confirmed for the window

4. Who does what

Role Task
Work lead (named in the plan; often the Shift lead or Facility manager) Runs the briefing, gives the go at each hold point, calls stop.
Cooling specialist (vendor) Does the maintenance, following the manufacturer’s procedure.
Electrical responsible person Isolates the unit, locks and tags it, and removes the lock after the work.
Operator on duty Watches temperatures, alarms and unit status during the whole window. Logs the readings.
Change manager Approvals, notifications and records.
Security Lets the vendor in, and knows who is in the data hall.

Staffing:

  • No one on site: never run this work. Someone from operations is on site for the whole window.
  • One person: usually fine when the vendor does the work and one operator watches the room, if the site’s rules allow it. That operator does nothing else during the window.
  • Two or more: needed for breaker switching or other high-risk work, which includes the electrical isolation, or when the room is close to its limits.
  • Security: told in advance, lets the vendor in, and knows who is in the data hall.

5. Steps

Step Action Check Owner
1 Brief everyone: the unit, the sequence, the stop conditions, and how long it takes to return the unit to service. Everyone can say their own task and who calls stop. Work lead
2 Confirm the change is approved and nothing else is reducing redundancy. No other work on cooling or power. Work lead, Change manager
3 Note the starting point in monitoring: inlet temperatures in the area, humidity, alarms, which units run and which wait. Baseline recorded. Operator on duty
4 Check the standby unit. Normally it starts on its own when the unit stops. Starting it by hand first is a decision for each case. In automatic, no alarms, ready to start. Operator on duty
5 Identify the unit at the unit itself. Tag matches the work approval. Checked by two people. Work lead, Cooling specialist
6 Hold point: go only when steps 1–5 are done and temperatures are stable. Work lead gives the go. Work lead
7 Stop the unit and put it in its maintenance state, following the manufacturer’s procedure. Unit stopped. Status shown correctly in monitoring. Cooling specialist
8 Watch the cooling take over. The standby unit starts as the control system is designed to. Temperatures stay stable. Operator on duty
9 Isolate the unit electrically, then lock and tag it. On chilled-water units, close the water valves only if the work needs it. Isolation confirmed. Lock and tag in place. Valve positions recorded. Electrical responsible person, Cooling specialist
10 Suppress alarms for this unit only, and log it. Only this unit’s alarms suppressed. Logged with time. Operator on duty
11 Watch temperatures for the agreed time before the work starts. Stable and within limits. Operator on duty
12 Do the maintenance. Read temperatures at the agreed interval. Work done as planned. Readings logged. Cooling specialist, Operator on duty
13 Hold point: before the unit goes back, check the work is finished. Panels closed. Tools and materials out. Valves in their normal position. No leaks. Work lead
14 Remove the lock and tag, restore power, and return the unit to service, following the manufacturer’s procedure. Unit running. Electrical responsible person, Cooling specialist
15 Watch the unit run. It behaves as intended: no alarms, air flowing, no leaks, condensate draining, no unusual noise. Cooling specialist, Operator on duty
16 Remove the alarm suppression for this unit. Alarms active again. Monitoring shows the unit normal. Operator on duty
17 Return the standby unit to standby, if it was started by hand. Standby unit in automatic and ready. Operator on duty
18 Watch temperatures for the agreed time. Back to the baseline from step 3. Operator on duty
19 Hold point: before closing, check the room is as it was. Unit in automatic, not local. Standby ready. No alarms suppressed. Containment doors closed, floor tiles in place, nothing in the air paths. Work lead
20 Record the work, parts, readings and findings, and close the change. Record complete. Work lead, Change manager

Note: The observation times in steps 11 and 18, and the temperature limits, are set for each case and building, from experience. Put them in the plan so nobody has to guess on the day.

6. If it goes wrong

Anyone can call stop. The aim is to keep the room within its limits, not to finish the work.

The standby unit doesn’t start on its own when the unit stops: don’t start the work. Return the unit to service and find out why. The same fault would leave the room without its spare at night, when nobody is there to start a unit by hand.

Temperatures climb toward the limit, or another unit fails: stop the work. If the unit can be returned safely within the known time, return it. If not, follow Cooling Failure Response (EOP-CLG-002).

A leak during the work: close the valves if it is safe to do so, keep away from electrical equipment in the wet area, and call the Shift lead. Follow Water Leak Response (EOP-WTR-003).

The unit won’t return to service: keep the standby unit running. The vendor finds the fault, and the Shift lead decides whether the window is extended. Until the unit is back, the room has lost redundancy, and it is escalated.

The unit returns but doesn’t behave as intended (alarms, weak cooling, leaks, unusual noise): take it out of service again, keep the standby unit running, and handle it as a unit that won’t return.

The wrong unit was stopped: restart it at once if it is safe, stop the work, and check temperatures before anything else.

Someone is hurt: people first. Call for help and follow the site’s own plan.

7. After the work

  • ☐ Unit in automatic and running or on standby as designed
  • ☐ Standby unit back on standby and ready
  • ☐ Lock and tag removed, valves in their normal position
  • ☐ No alarms suppressed
  • ☐ Temperatures back to the baseline
  • ☐ Containment and floor tiles in place, area clean
  • ☐ Work, parts and findings recorded, change closed

8. Rules and standards

ASHRAE TC 9.9 recommends an inlet temperature of 18–27 °C for most IT equipment. In Europe, the F-gas Regulation means only certified people may work on refrigerant circuits. Electrical isolation with lock and tag is standard practice and follows the local rules: EN 50110-1 in Europe, FSE in Norway, the Electricity at Work Regulations 1989 in the UK and NFPA 70E in the US. The manufacturer’s instructions set the maintenance tasks and the start and stop sequence.
