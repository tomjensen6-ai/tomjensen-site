Operational Governance

A procedure only helps if people can trust it: current, approved and owned.

Purpose

How controlled documents move from draft to approval, review, change and retirement.

Why governance matters

An old procedure is dangerous because people trust it.

The failures are simple. Someone follows an old revision. Nobody knows who can approve a change. The equipment was replaced, but the procedure wasn't.

Governance answers four questions for every document: who owns it, who approves it, which revision is valid, and when it is reviewed next.

Document ownership

Every document has a named owner. A role is fine, but it has to be one role. A document nobody owns goes out of date.

  • Document owner: keeps it correct, starts the review and logs changes.
  • Reviewer: checks the technical content and that the steps work on site.
  • Approver: releases it for use.
  • Users: follow the approved revision and report what is wrong or missing.

Approval

The operations team approves its own documents. A document for work with High or Critical risk also needs approval from the Facility manager.

Document What the approver checks
SOP The routine is clear, safe and can be repeated
MOP Risk, customer impact, hold points and rollback
EOP Safety, escalation and the first actions
Risk assessment Hazards, controls and the risk left after controls

Revisions

A minor change is a typo, a phone number or a clearer word. The owner fixes it and logs it in the revision table inside the document.

A major change is new steps, new equipment, changed roles or changed risk. It goes through review and approval again, like a new document.

Every controlled document shows:

  • Document ID
  • Title
  • Revision
  • Status
  • Owner
  • Approval date
  • Next review date
  • Revision log
  • Classification
Revision Change By Approval
1.0 First approved issue Document owner Approver
1.1 Phone number updated Document owner Logged, no new approval
2.0 New steps after UPS replacement Document owner Approver, after full review

Document IDs are never reused. A retired document leaves a gap in the numbers, so an old reference never points to the wrong procedure.

Document status

  • Draft: being written. Not for use.
  • For review: with the reviewers.
  • Approved: in use.
  • Retired: no longer valid. If it was replaced, the log names the new document.

The approved revision stays in use while the next one is being written.

Draft → Review → Approval → In use → Yearly review → Revision or retirement

Review cycle

Every SOP, MOP and EOP is reviewed once a year. An EOP is also reviewed after it has been used.

Other things start a review early: replaced equipment, a changed layout, an incident or near miss, an audit finding, or users who keep asking the same question.

Printed copies

Emergency procedures must work when screens don't. They are printed and kept where they are used, for example in the control room.

A printed copy is marked “Uncontrolled when printed”. One person swaps the printed copies when a new revision is approved and removes the old ones.

Classification

Each document has a label, so people know who can see it and where it can be stored.

  • Public: can be shared outside the organization.
  • Internal: for staff and contractors working on site.
  • Restricted: asset details, layouts or access information.
  • Confidential: the most sensitive content, shared only with named people.

A label set too high makes a document hard to reach. A label set too low exposes the site.

Authority

Decide who decides before work starts, not during a change window or an incident.

  • Anyone on site: can stop unsafe work they see.
  • Incident lead: leads the response during an incident.
  • Change manager: approves planned changes.
  • Facility manager: approves documents and work with High or Critical risk.

Stopped work does not just carry on. Before it starts again, everyone involved reads the instructions through again and signs off.

The Incident lead runs the response, not the tools. Technical tasks go to others, so someone keeps the overview and handles communication.

Before approval

Before a document is approved, check that:

  • The owner is named
  • The type is clear: SOP, MOP, EOP or risk assessment
  • The scope is clear
  • Assets and locations are named with tags
  • Roles are assigned
  • Stop conditions and escalation are included where needed
  • Rollback or recovery is included where relevant
  • Someone who knows the system has reviewed it
  • Revision and status are visible
  • The next review date is set

Rules and standards

ISO 9001 and ISO/IEC 27001 both cover document control in clause 7.5, documented information. Documents must be identified, reviewed and approved before use, kept under version control, and available where and when they are needed. Local law and the site's own management system come on top.
