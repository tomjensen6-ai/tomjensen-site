Cooling System Inspection

How I would do the routine walk-round of data hall cooling. It catches the quiet problems: a standby unit that isn’t ready, a growing hot spot, a drain that has started to leak.

Classification

An example I wrote for this site. A real inspection must follow the site design, the manufacturer’s instructions and local safety rules.

Document ID: SOP-CLG-004
Revision: 2.0
Status: Example
Approval: Example only

1. Purpose

The inspection checks that the cooling can still do its job, with the redundancy the design expects: units in automatic, standby units ready, temperatures within limits at the IT equipment, air going where it should, and no water where it shouldn’t be.

It does not replace maintenance. Filters, coils, belts, valves and refrigerant circuits are serviced on their own schedule by people trained for it.

2. The real risk

One high risk is losing redundancy without noticing. A standby unit is switched off after service. A unit is left in local mode, so the control system can’t start it. A unit runs with an alarm nobody has acted on. Nothing looks wrong until a running unit fails and the standby doesn’t take over.

Airflow is another. Missing blanking panels, open floor tiles, unsealed cable openings and propped containment doors let cold air bypass the equipment. The units work harder and hot spots appear, even with enough cooling installed.

Water is a third. Condensate drains block, pumps fail, and pipe joints weep. Water under a raised floor can reach power cables before anyone sees it.

The risk to you: fans can start without warning, panels and units can be live, and open floor tiles are a fall hazard. Never put your hands inside a unit, and don’t lift floor tiles on your own.

3. Before you start

  • ☐ The inspection is on the schedule, and the Shift lead knows you are going
  • ☐ No work going on with the cooling units or pipework in the area
  • ☐ Access to the data halls and technical rooms on the route
  • ☐ A torch, and a handheld thermometer if the site allows one
  • ☐ Last time’s record at hand, so you can compare
  • ☐ Monitoring available, so you can compare what it shows with what you see

4. Who does what

Role Task
Operator on duty Does the inspection, records the findings, reports anything abnormal at once.
Shift lead Reviews findings, decides what is urgent, calls the vendor if needed.
Facility manager Owns the schedule and the records. Follows up open findings.
Cooling specialist (vendor) Fixes faults outside the operator’s scope. Only certified technicians work on refrigerant circuits.
Security Knows who is in the data halls.

Staffing:

  • No one on site: the walk-round waits until someone is. Check temperatures and unit status in monitoring if you can, and record that the inspection was missed.
  • One person: fine for the visual check, if the site’s rules allow it. On large sites the round is often split between several people, each with their own area. Tell the Shift lead or Security when you start and when you finish. Do nothing beyond looking, reading displays and taking temperatures.
  • Two or more: needed for anything beyond the visual check, such as lifting floor tiles or opening unit panels.
  • Security: knows who is in the data halls and for how long.

5. Steps

Step Action Check Owner
1 Tell the Shift lead you are starting. Look at monitoring first: room temperatures, humidity, alarms, and which units are running and which are on standby. Status noted before you go in. Operator on duty
2 Walk the route through the data halls and technical rooms. Exits and service clearances clear. Nothing stored in front of units. Operator on duty
3 Check each cooling unit’s display. In automatic, not local or manual. No alarms. Running or on standby as monitoring shows. Operator on duty
4 Check the standby units. In automatic, no alarm, ready to start. A standby unit that is switched off is lost redundancy. Operator on duty
5 Look at the air intake and discharge of each unit. Nothing blocking them. Operator on duty
6 Walk the cold aisles. Read the inlet temperatures at the front of the racks, from the rack sensors or a handheld thermometer. Within the site’s limits. No rack noticeably warmer than its neighbors. Operator on duty
7 Check the containment and air paths. Containment doors closed. Floor tiles in place, perforated tiles only where planned. Blanking panels in place, cable openings sealed. Gaps in customer racks are recorded and reported to the customer. Operator on duty
8 Check visible pipework, valves, drains and condensate pumps, and the leak detection cable. No leaks, drips or standing water. Drains clear. No pump alarm. Leak detection cable in place and undamaged. Operator on duty
9 Look at filters and grilles where site practice allows. Read the filter pressure indicator if one is fitted. No heavy dust or damage. Indicator in its normal range. Operator on duty
10 Listen to each unit. No unusual noise, vibration or rattling. Operator on duty
11 Compare what you see with monitoring. Local displays and readings match monitoring. Operator on duty
12 Hold point: before you leave, check that everything is as you found it. Units in automatic. No alarms caused by the round. Containment doors closed. Floor tiles in place. Operator on duty
13 Record temperatures, humidity, which units run and which are on standby, and your findings. Compare with last time. Record complete. Changes since last time noted. Operator on duty
14 Report findings. Anything that reduces cooling capacity or redundancy goes to the Shift lead at once, by phone, not only in the log. Each finding has an owner. Operator on duty, Shift lead

Note: What counts as normal (temperature limits, humidity range, which units run and which wait) comes from the site design and the cooling control strategy. Put the numbers in the site’s own checklist so nobody has to guess.

6. If you find something wrong

If anything suggests the site has less cooling or less redundancy than it should, say so at once.

A unit in local or manual mode, switched off, or a standby unit not ready: don’t just put it back. Someone may be working on it. Tell the Shift lead, who finds out why and decides who returns it to automatic. Until then it is lost redundancy, and it is escalated.

Active alarm: record it and don’t reset it without knowing the cause. The Shift lead calls the vendor if it affects capacity.

Temperatures above the site’s limit, or a clear hot spot: call the Shift lead at once. Check whether a unit has stopped. If cooling capacity is lost, follow Cooling Failure Response (EOP-CLG-002).

Missing blanking panels or open gaps in a customer rack: record it and tell the customer. The site’s rules usually require blanking panels, and fitting them is normally the customer’s cost. Operations often fits them in the end, once it is agreed with the customer.

Water, a leak or standing water: keep away from electrical equipment in the wet area. Find the source only if it is safe to do so, and call the Shift lead. Follow Water Leak Response (EOP-WTR-003).

Refrigerant: oil stains on pipe joints or a hissing sound. Don’t touch anything and call the Shift lead. Only a certified technician works on the circuit.

Someone is hurt: people first. Call for help and follow the site’s own plan.

7. After the inspection

  • ☐ All units in automatic, no new alarms
  • ☐ Containment doors closed, floor tiles in place, route clear
  • ☐ Record complete and compared with last time
  • ☐ Findings reported, each with an owner
  • ☐ Shift lead told the round is finished

8. Rules and standards

ASHRAE TC 9.9 publishes the thermal guidelines most data centers use. For most IT equipment, the recommended inlet temperature is 18–27 °C. EN 50600-2-3 covers environmental control in data centers. In Europe, the F-gas Regulation means only certified people may work on refrigerant circuits. The site’s design and the manufacturer’s instructions set the actual limits and service intervals.
