Generator Weekly Inspection

How I would do the weekly walk-round of a standby generator. It takes a short time, and it catches the simple things that stop a generator from starting when the grid fails.

Classification

An example I wrote for this site. A real inspection must follow the manufacturer's instructions, the site design and local safety rules.

Document ID: SOP-GEN-002
Revision: 2.0
Status: Example
Approval: Example only

1. Purpose

A standby generator spends almost all its life waiting. It runs only for tests and maintenance, but it must always be ready to start. The weekly inspection checks that it is: in automatic, no alarms, fuel in the tank, engine warm, batteries charged, air path clear.

It does not replace running the generator. A generator can look fine and still fail to start or take load. Only a test run shows that, so most sites also run their generators on a regular schedule.

2. The real risk

A generator can be left in a state where it won't start on its own: switch in manual or off, emergency stop pressed, a breaker open, or the battery charger off. This often happens after maintenance, and nothing looks wrong from the outside. Two things help. Test-run the generator after every maintenance before you call it ready. And make every switch that can stop an automatic start raise an alarm in monitoring. The weekly walk-round is the third check.

Small faults also grow. Starting batteries are the part most likely to fail over a generator's life, so many sites replace them on a fixed cycle instead of waiting for a failure. A failed engine heater leaves the engine cold and slow to start. A slow fuel leak. Water in the tank bund. A blocked air intake.

A generator that isn't ready to start is lost redundancy, even when the grid is fine. Treat it that way.

The risk to you: a standby generator can start at any moment, without warning, if the grid fails or someone starts a test. Stay clear of fans, belts and the exhaust. Wear hearing protection in the room. Keep your hands away from moving parts, even when the engine stands still.

3. Before you start

  • ☐ The inspection is on the schedule, and the Shift lead knows you are going
  • ☐ No work or test going on with the generators or the power system
  • ☐ Access to the generator room; on high-voltage sites, documented access for that room
  • ☐ Hearing protection, gloves and a torch
  • ☐ Last week's record at hand, so you can compare
  • ☐ Monitoring available, so you can compare what it shows with what you see

4. Who does what

Role Task
Operator on duty Does the inspection, records the findings, reports anything abnormal at once.
Shift lead Reviews findings, decides what is urgent, calls the vendor if needed.
Facility manager Owns the schedule and the records. Follows up open findings.
Generator specialist (vendor) Fixes faults outside the operator's scope.
Security Knows who is in the generator area.

Staffing:

  • No one on site: the walk-round waits until someone is. Check the generator status in monitoring if you can, and record that the inspection was missed.
  • One person: fine for the visual check on a low-voltage generator, if the site's safety rules allow it, there are clear instructions at the generator (signs, a QR code to the checklist) and the round is signed off. Tell the Shift lead or Security when you go in and when you come out. The room is loud, so you may not hear a radio. Do nothing beyond looking and reading gauges.
  • Two or more: needed for anything beyond the visual check, such as opening covers or work on batteries.
  • High and medium voltage: where generators run above 1000 V AC, always two people, and only people with documented access to the room. Operators and security are not always among them. Plan the weekly round so it is done by people who are allowed in.
  • Security: knows who is in the generator area and for how long.

5. Steps

Step Action Check Owner
1 Tell the Shift lead you are starting. Look at the generator in monitoring first. Status and alarms noted before you go in. Operator on duty
2 Walk the route to the generator room or enclosure. Route, doors and exits clear. Nothing stored in the room. Operator on duty
3 Look over the room or enclosure. No water coming in, no leaks under the engine, no fuel smell, no damage. Operator on duty
4 Check the controller panel. In automatic. No alarms or warnings. Emergency stop released. Operator on duty
5 Look at the generator breaker and any isolators. Look only. In their normal position. Operator on duty
6 Check the engine heater. Heater on. Coolant temperature in the normal range for standby. Operator on duty
7 Check coolant and oil on sight glasses or gauges. Use the dipstick only where the site's instructions allow it. Levels within the marks. Oil not milky. Operator on duty
8 Check the starting batteries and charger. Charger on, no alarm. No corrosion, swelling or leaks. Voltage noted. Battery age within the site's replacement cycle. Operator on duty
9 Check the fuel: tank levels, transfer pumps, visible lines and valves, the bund. Level above the site minimum. Pumps in automatic. Valves in their normal position. No leaks. Bund dry. Operator on duty
10 Check the air intake, louvres and exhaust. Louvres or dampers in automatic and free to open. Intake clear. No soot or damage on the exhaust. Operator on duty
11 Compare what you see with monitoring. Local readings match monitoring. Operator on duty
12 Hold point: before you leave, look at the controller panel once more. In automatic. No alarms. Emergency stop released. Breaker in its normal position. Operator on duty
13 Record running hours, fuel level, battery voltage, coolant temperature and findings. Compare with last week. Record complete. Changes since last week noted. Running hours that went up with no test or outage are a finding. Operator on duty
14 Report findings. Anything that could stop the generator starting goes to the Shift lead at once, by phone, not only in the log. Each finding has an owner. Operator on duty, Shift lead

Note: What counts as normal (coolant temperature, battery voltage, fuel minimum) comes from the manufacturer and the site design. Put the numbers in the site's own checklist so nobody has to guess.

6. If you find something wrong

If anything suggests the generator might not start, say so at once. Treat it as not available until someone qualified says otherwise.

Not in automatic, emergency stop pressed, or breaker open: don't just put it back. Someone may be working on it. Tell the Shift lead, who finds out why and decides who returns it to automatic. Record it either way.

Active alarm: record it and don't reset it without knowing the cause. If it could affect starting, the Shift lead calls the vendor.

Generator not available: this is lost redundancy, and it is escalated. The Shift lead tells the Facility manager and the On-call person, stops planned work that relies on the generators, and postpones any power tests.

Fuel leak or strong fuel smell: no sparks or open flame. Close the valve if it is safe to do so, contain the spill with the spill kit, and call the Shift lead. If the leak is large or there is a fire risk, leave and raise the alarm. See Fire Alarm Response (EOP-FIR-004).

The generator starts while you are in the room: step back from moving parts, leave calmly, and call the Shift lead. A start you didn't expect may mean the grid has failed. See Utility Power Loss Response (EOP-PWR-001).

Someone is hurt: people first. Call for help and follow the site's own plan.

7. After the inspection

  • ☐ Generator in automatic, no new alarms
  • ☐ Room or enclosure closed and secured, route clear
  • ☐ Record complete and compared with last week
  • ☐ Findings reported, each with an owner
  • ☐ Shift lead told you are out of the generator area

8. Rules and standards

What is required varies by country and by site. In the US, NFPA 110 (Standard for Emergency and Standby Power Systems) requires a weekly inspection and a monthly run under load for emergency and legally required systems (Level 1 and Level 2). Many data center generators are optional standby, so there NFPA 110 is often used as good practice rather than as a legal requirement. In Norway, no rule sets a weekly check; the schedule follows the manufacturer's recommendations. Elsewhere, check local rules and the insurer's requirements. Rules for safe access and electrical work still apply: EN 50110-1 in Europe, FSE in Norway, the Electricity at Work Regulations 1989 in the UK and NFPA 70E in the US.
