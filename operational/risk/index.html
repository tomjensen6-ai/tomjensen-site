Risk Assessment

How I would assess the risk of a task before the work starts. The score is the easy part. The value is in agreeing what could go wrong, what you will do about it, and who accepts what is left.

1. When to do it

Do a written risk assessment for any planned work on live power, cooling or fire systems, any work that reduces redundancy, and any task the team hasn't done before. Do it again when the plan changes, after an incident, and when the same task goes wrong somewhere else.

The people who will do the work take part. At least one person who won't do the work reviews it. Someone who only wants the work done should never be the one who accepts the risk.

2. The steps

The method follows the process in ISO 31000, the international guideline for risk management:

  • Describe the work: what, where, when, and which systems are affected.
  • Find what can go wrong: for people, for the load, for the equipment, and the cost.
  • Score each risk: likelihood and consequence (sannsynlighet og konsekvens).
  • Decide: is the risk acceptable as it is?
  • Add controls, and score again.
  • Get the remaining risk accepted by the right person.
  • Watch for changes during the work, and stop if the picture changes.

3. Likelihood

How likely is it that this happens during this work, or in this period?

Level Likelihood What it means
1 Very unlikely Not seen in this kind of work. It would take several failures at once.
2 Unlikely It has happened in the industry, but nobody expects it here.
3 Possible It has happened on sites like this one. It could happen this time.
4 Likely Expected at some point if nothing changes.
5 Almost certain Already happening, or will happen without action.

4. Consequence

Score all four columns and use the worst one.

Level People Service and load Equipment Cost
1 No injury No effect No damage Negligible
2 First aid One redundant component lost, no load affected Minor repair Covered by the normal operating budget
3 Medical treatment A system runs without redundancy, or margins near their limits (temperature, battery time) Component replaced Needs approval outside the budget
4 Serious injury Load dropped in any room, critical or not, or for some customers Major unit damaged, long repair Large unplanned cost, or contract penalties
5 Fatality or life-changing injury Load dropped across the site or for many customers Major system destroyed, or fire Threatens the contract, the customer or the business

Note: The site sets the amounts for each cost level. Cost alone can raise the consequence: a repair bill or contract penalty at level 4 makes it a 4, even if no load was lost.

5. The matrix

Likelihood × consequence gives a score from 1 to 25. It's a way to rank risks, not a measurement, so the levels matter more than the numbers.

Severity rule: a consequence of 5 is never below High, however unlikely it is. Without this rule, an event that could drop the whole site scores the same as a near-certain nuisance. That is the best-known weakness of risk matrices. (* marks the cell this rule raises.)

Level Score What happens next
Low 1–4 Go ahead with normal controls. The Shift lead can accept.
Medium 5–9 Go ahead with the listed controls and a named owner for each. The Facility manager accepts.
High 10–16, or any consequence 5 Only with controls in place, hold points and stop conditions. The risk owner, often the Site manager or above, accepts in writing before the work starts.
Critical 17–25 Don't start. Change the plan until the risk is lower. If it can't be lowered, the decision goes to the highest management level.

Note: Who may accept each level is set by the site's own governance. The roles above are an example.

6. Controls: what actually reduces risk

Controls are not equal. From strongest to weakest:

  • Remove the hazard: don't do the work on a live system, or do it another way.
  • Design: redundancy, interlocks, a second supply.
  • Procedures: a MOP, hold points, a second person checking the asset tag.
  • Protective equipment: arc-flash gear, gloves, hearing protection.

Most procedure controls only make an event less likely. Very few make it less serious. If the load drops, a good MOP doesn't make it drop more gently. So after controls, likelihood usually goes down and consequence often stays the same. Be suspicious of an assessment where consequence drops because of paperwork.

Only count a control that is in place on the day. "Generators will be checked" is a plan. "Generators checked, in automatic, fuel above minimum" is a control.

7. The exposure window

The exposure window is the time the site is more vulnerable than normal: from when redundancy is reduced until it is back. It often matters more than how long the task itself takes. Keep it short, and plan the work so it can stop safely at any point inside it.

Exposure Example
Power UPS on maintenance bypass, a generator out of service
Cooling A cooling unit stopped, less thermal margin
Monitoring Alarms suppressed, a sensor out of service
Recovery No quick way back if something fails
People Fewer people on site, a handover during the work

8. Stop conditions

Agree before the work starts when it stops. Without that, people keep going because they have already started.

Stop and reassess when:

  • conditions don't match the approved plan
  • redundancy becomes uncertain
  • an unexpected alarm appears
  • the asset or its state can't be confirmed
  • the way back is no longer available
  • anyone is unsure

If the risk picture changes, stop first and reassess. Continuing is a new decision, not the default.

9. Worked examples

Example 1

UPS to maintenance bypass (MOP-UPS-001)

Risk: the utility fails while the load is on maintenance bypass. The UPS can't carry the load, so the IT load drops until the generators take over.

Before controls

High

Likelihood 2, consequence 5. Score 10, High.

Controls

Work in a planned window, with no storms forecast and no known grid work; generators checked and in automatic; the exposure window kept as short as possible; the switching sequence rehearsed so the way back is quick.

After controls

High

Likelihood 1, consequence 5. Score 5, still High under the severity rule.

Why it can't go lower: the consequence is set by the design. While the UPS is on maintenance bypass, only the generators stand between a utility failure and the load, and they need time to start. Only a second supply path would lower the consequence, and a procedure can't add one. The work can't be skipped either, because the UPS needs maintenance.

Decision: the risk owner accepts a High risk knowingly, in writing, for the shortest possible window, with the stop conditions in the MOP.

Note: On a site where every IT device has two supplies from separate UPS systems, the same work scores consequence 3, because the other side carries the load.

Example 2

Planned utility transfer test (MOP-PWR-002)

Risk: cooling is slow to restart after the transfer to generator, and data hall temperatures pass their limit.

Before controls

Medium

Likelihood 3, consequence 3. Score 9, Medium.

Controls

Pumps and CRAH fans on their own UPS; chiller restart time known from the last test; the test done in cool weather; a temperature limit agreed as a stop condition.

After controls

Medium

Likelihood 2, consequence 3. Score 6, Medium.

Decision: the Facility manager accepts. The same test also carries a High risk (the automation fails and the way back depends on manual switching), assessed on its own line.

Example 3

Vendor work in a data hall (SOP-SEC-003)

Risk: the vendor works on the wrong rack or pulls the wrong cable, and drops customer equipment.

Before controls

High

Likelihood 3, consequence 4. Score 12, High.

Controls

The work approval names the rack and asset tags; the label is checked against the approval before anything is touched; an escort from operations stays for work on critical equipment.

After controls

Medium

Likelihood 2, consequence 4. Score 8, Medium.

Decision: the Facility manager accepts.

10. Common mistakes

  • Scoring to fit the plan: the score is decided first, the reasons afterwards.
  • Counting controls that aren't in place yet.
  • Lowering consequence with procedures instead of design.
  • Leaving out cost: contract penalties and repair bills are consequences too.
  • One generic assessment reused for every job.
  • Accepting the risk yourself because you want the work done.
  • Never looking at it again after the work has started.
